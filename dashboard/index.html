Workpaper A1 · cost reconciliation
Bounded below — a floor, not an estimate

The $42M is not the problem. It is the part Finance can already see.

Apply Finance's own unit costs — unchanged, straight from the brief — to the population actually present in the data, and the figure lands at $143.7M. Nothing was re-derived and no new assumption was introduced. The gap is entirely a headcount question: Finance costed roughly the right number of regrettable exits and roughly a fifth of the disengaged and agency-hired staff who are sitting in the file.

And it is a floor twice over. The attrition log accounts for 60% of the departures the annual report claims for FY2025, so the exits driving the largest component are undercounted before the arithmetic starts.

What to defend, and what to concede

Defend: $143.7M is Finance's arithmetic, not ours. Every constant is theirs. Concede the range before someone asks for it — the disengagement component moves between $10.9M and $165.3M on the threshold alone.

Concede: the log covers 60% of the FY2025 departures the annual report reports. That understates the regrettable-attrition component specifically, which is the one component that currently reconciles. Resolve the reconciliation before the number is quoted externally.

Workpaper A2 · the FAR natural experiment
Not identified — this is not the same as "no effect"

FAR did not push regulated staff out. Nor can this data show that it did.

The Financial Accountability Regime commenced for banking on 15 March 2024 and for insurance and superannuation on 15 March 2025. That twelve-month stagger is the closest thing to a randomised trial a people dataset ever offers, and it is the right instinct to reach for it.

It does not survive contact with the observation window. The data begins in January 2024 — six weeks before the first wave commenced. The obvious before/after comparison returns a 9× jump that is purely an artefact of measuring 1.4 months against nine. Run the design properly and the estimate is +1.4 exits per 1,000, against a standard error of 14.2.

Three estimates of the same thing, on one axis

Change in exits per 1,000 regulated employees, attributable to FAR. An interval is drawn only where the design supports computing one.

Zero sits comfortably inside the only interval this data supports. The two within-wave estimates disagree in sign, which is not a contradiction — both are noise around nothing. 476 regulated employees producing 39 exits cannot resolve an effect of any plausible size.

Why the naive answer is 9×

Months of data either side of each wave's commencement. The dataset opens on 1 January 2024; FAR commenced for banking on 15 March 2024.

The orange block is the entire Wave 1 pre-period. A ratio computed against six weeks measures the length of the window, not the effect of the regulation. Lead the presentation with this — producing the 9× and then dismantling it is a stronger opening than any finding on the screen.

Three reasons not to spend on this

Each is independently sufficient to stop a FAR-attributed retention programme from being funded.

A competing explanation fits better. The Entity_B and Entity_C acquisitions run through the same window. Among regulated staff, Entity_B leaves at 17.1% against Entity_A's 8.0%. Integration is at least as plausible as regulation, and it is actionable.

The population where the effect should be strongest is unmeasurable. FAR attaches to accountable persons, so the effect should concentrate in senior regulated roles — of which there are 22 in Wave 1 and 6 in Wave 2. One departure moves the rate by five points.

230 of the most exposed people are missing from the cut entirely. Executive Leadership maps to no wave, because it spans both regulated arms. They are reported separately rather than dropped, but no wave-level statistic includes them.

The distinction that matters

"Not identified" is not "no effect." The interval runs from −26.4 to +29.2 per 1,000. A real and material FAR exodus is inside that interval; so is a real and material retention effect. The correct statement to a board is that this dataset cannot distinguish them — and that a claim in either direction is unsupported, including the one everyone expected to find.

Workpaper A3 · non-response as a leading indicator
Identified — corrected for exposure, holds

The people who stop answering are the ones who leave.

An employee who answered the engagement survey and then goes quiet for two consecutive waves leaves at 2.2× the rate of their peers. The signal HR watches today — a low engagement score — carries a lift of 1.07×. Silence is roughly twice as predictive as the thing currently being monitored, and it is free.

It also arrives sooner and reaches further. A score-based alert needs a processed survey cycle and can only fire for somebody who answered. Non-response is known the day the wave closes, and it fires precisely on the population that has no score at all.

The 4.87× is real but thin — 63 people, once exposure is properly controlled. Build the alert on went silent, which carries a defensible sample and the operational advantage: it fires the day the wave closes, with no scoring cycle in between.

The gap is a level, not a collapse

Survey response rate by how long before their exit the survey was issued. Leavers sit ~15 points below stayers from at least 18 months out, and never recover.

There is no late collapse, and that is the finding. A gap that opens 18 months out and holds flat means the mechanism is disengagement, not notice-period checkout — and it means the intervention window is measured in quarters, not weeks.

The correction that makes it real

The number an ordinary analysis produces is wrong by more than half

Run straight, "never responded" appears to carry a 2.9× lift. That is survey exposure, not disengagement: Entity_C joined the survey platform at wave 5, so a single missed email makes 1,014 people look like career non-responders.

Entity_C's 31.4% non-response sits with the second-lowest attrition in the company. The apparent signal tracked how many surveys people were sent, not how they felt. Conditioning on three or more waves issued — 11,518 employees, 85.9% of the company — removes the artefact and leaves a 2.16× lift that holds.

What this cannot tell you

New starters are outside the estimate. Requiring three waves of exposure removes almost everyone hired in the last year — and that is the population with both the highest non-response and the highest attrition. The 2.16× is clean within a long-tenured population and silent about the group a CHRO most wants to watch. A person-wave hazard model recovers them, and is the first thing to build next.

It is not a proxy for something already known. Silence does not track attrition across tenure bands or legacy entities — Entity_B has the highest silence rate at 10.5% and the lowest attrition at 4.4% within this population — yet it predicts at the individual level. That is what makes it worth deploying rather than absorbing into an existing segment cut.

Fix three data defects, in this order

The attrition log. 1,400 rows over 24 months against an annual report claiming 1,400 departures in FY2025 alone. Until this reconciles, every cost figure stays a floor and no attrition rate can be quoted externally.

Survey exposure for acquired entities. Entity_C was surveyed once. Any analysis of response behaviour that does not condition on waves issued is out by a factor of two.
